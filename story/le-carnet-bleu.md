# Le Carnet Bleu

## A mystery in ten chapters

### Chapter I — The House Above the Sea

The road to Maison Bleue climbed out of the village in seven narrow turns, each more unreasonable than the last.

By the fourth, Hélène de Veyrac had ceased to look through the motorcar window. There was nothing to see but rain running silver across the glass and, at intervals, the white violence of the sea below. The driver bent over the wheel as though in prayer.

“Madame,” he said, when the house at last appeared, “I do not think the lower road will remain open.”

“Then we shall hope,” said Hélène, “that Monsieur Valère has improved his cellar.”

Maison Bleue stood alone on the headland, a square pale house with dark blue shutters. It had belonged to her husband once. In those days it had been called the Villa Saint-Auban and there had been servants in the attics, gardeners in the lower cottages, and a sapphire in a locked cabinet in the blue salon.

All three had gone.

Armand Valère opened the door before the bell sounded. At sixty-two he was still called the Concierge, although there was no longer anyone above him to serve. He had bought the house cheaply after the Marquis’s death and preserved in his manner the exquisite attentiveness of a servant who knows his employers’ secrets.

“Madame la Marquise.”

“Do not call me that.”

“Forgive me. Old entries are difficult to correct.”

His eyes rested briefly upon the silver paper knife she carried with her correspondence case. Its sheath was blue velvet, worn pale at the edges. The handle bore the joined initials H.V.

“You asked me to bring it,” she said.

“I asked each of you to bring one object from that night.”

“And if I had refused?”

“You did not.”

He took her coat. The entrance hall smelled of beeswax, damp stone, and the violets she wore at her throat.

There were already voices in the drawing room.

Pierre Mercier stood before the cold fireplace with his old police watch open in his palm. Retirement had reduced him without softening him. His blue suit was sober, his shoes meticulously polished, his grey moustache clipped to the dimensions of a regulation.

Beside the window was Amélie Renaud, once the most celebrated singer at the Mirador and now, if the newspapers were to be believed, a woman who accepted only engagements that amused her. Her dress was midnight blue. A small handkerchief of the same colour was tied around one wrist.

“Hélène,” she said. “You look exactly as you did at the trial.”

“You do not.”

“No. I have had the advantage of becoming honest.”

Pierre closed his watch.

“We agreed,” he said, “that nothing would be gained by provocation.”

“Did we?” Amélie asked. “How industrious of us.”

The door opened again and François Bellande entered without waiting to be announced.

He was a tall, quiet man of thirty-six. Flour had worked permanently into the fine lines of his hands, although he wore an evening suit and had closed his pâtisserie for the occasion. In the breast pocket of his coat was a yellow envelope bearing the stamp of the prison at Toulon.

Hélène looked at the envelope. Then she looked at his face.

“You have his eyes,” she said.

“People generally mention the conviction first.”

No one spoke after that.

The fifth guest arrived precisely at eight.

Jacques Vallon came in smiling, rainless beneath a black umbrella held by the driver he had brought from Marseille. Prosperity had suited him. His dinner clothes were immaculate, his gloves pale grey, and a silver carnation gleamed at his lapel. He had once been the Marquis de Veyrac’s private secretary. Now banks waited when he was late.

“My dear Armand,” he said, offering both hands. “You have assembled the dead.”

“Only their witnesses.”

Jacques’s smile did not alter.

Armand led them into the dining room.

Six places had been set beneath the low chandelier. At five were name cards. At the sixth, between François and Jacques, stood an empty chair.

Its card read:

**LUCIEN BELLANDE**

François stopped.

Hélène’s hand went to the pearls at her throat.

“That,” Pierre said sharply, “is in very poor taste.”

“On the contrary,” said Armand. “It is the first accurate seating plan this house has seen in sixteen years.”

Outside, somewhere below the cliff, stones gave way beneath the rain. The sound was soft and final.

The lower road had closed.

They were alone with the empty chair.

### Chapter II — A Toast to the Absent

Armand served the first course himself: a clear broth, almost without flavour. It seemed designed not to distract from fear.

On the sideboard stood a blue notebook.

It was neither large nor remarkable. Its cloth cover had faded at the corners, and a black elastic band held it shut. Yet each guest looked toward it and then carefully elsewhere.

Jacques was the first to speak naturally.

“I confess myself disappointed. I had expected accusations before the soup.”

“You were always impatient,” said Amélie.

“And you were always theatrical.”

“That is why only one of us was paid for it.”

Armand poured the wine. He placed before Jacques a small cedar cigar box tied with blue ribbon.

“A gift?” Jacques asked.

“A relic. It was on the card table in the blue salon that night.”

Jacques turned it in his gloved hands. “And is it to be opened?”

“Everything is to be opened.”

Hélène had laid her correspondence case beside her plate. The silver handle of the paper knife projected from its velvet sheath.

“May I?” Jacques asked her.

She gave him a long look. Then she drew out the paper knife and passed it across the table.

It was an ornamental thing, sharp enough for envelopes but too elegant for honest work. Jacques slipped it beneath the ribbon.

“Before we begin,” he said, rising, “I should like to propose a toast.”

He held his glass toward the empty chair.

“To Luc Bellande, whose empty place has waited sixteen years. And to our host, who has at last remembered that the dead may have questions.”

François did not lift his glass.

Pierre said, “Sit down, Vallon.”

But Armand was smiling.

“The question is simple,” Jacques continued. “Why are we here?”

“Because at midnight,” said Armand, “I shall read Le Carnet Bleu aloud.”

The rain struck the windows. No one moved.

Jacques lowered his glass. His right hand came to rest near Hélène’s place. There was a little movement of silver and blue velvet.

François saw the sheath beside Hélène’s glass.

Amélie saw Jacques close his left hand.

Hélène saw only that her property appeared to have been returned.

Armand took the notebook from the sideboard.

“For twenty-three years,” he said, “I recorded what happened in this house. Purchases. Visitors. Messages. Indiscretions. I found that the truth becomes more valuable when those concerned believe it has been lost.”

“You mean you blackmailed us,” said Pierre.

“You prefer the language of reports, Inspector. I kept an account.”

“Of what?” François asked.

“Of the Saint-Auban sapphire. Of the failure of the lights. Of your father’s arrest. Of the evidence that disappeared. Of the people who paid to keep it so.”

François removed the yellow envelope from his pocket and placed it beside the notebook.

“My father’s last letter,” he said. “He wrote it three days before he died.”

Armand did not touch it.

“Read it,” said François.

“I have already read it.”

“Then read the notebook.”

“At midnight.”

François produced a second paper: a telegram folded twice.

“An examining magistrate will be here at dawn. He will take the notebook whether you read it or not.”

Pierre’s head turned. “Which magistrate?”

“Delorme, at Aix.”

“I know Delorme.”

“Then you know he dislikes being kept waiting.”

Armand’s eyes passed from the telegram to François’s face. Something like approval appeared in them.

“Very good,” he said. “The son has learned to bluff.”

François went pale.

Hélène tore a page from her correspondence pad. Beneath the table she wrote a single sentence, folded it, and pushed it toward Pierre when Armand turned to fetch the second course.

Pierre opened it beneath his napkin.

**The west drawer. If I am delayed, open it before the police arrive.**

He looked at her.

She gave the smallest shake of her head.

At the sideboard, Armand opened Le Carnet Bleu.

“Let us begin,” he said, “with page forty-seven.”

### Chapter III — Page Forty-Seven

The page was ruled in blue pencil and covered in Armand’s narrow writing. The ink had once been violet-black; age had browned it without obscuring the words.

At the top was written:

**J. VALLON LEFT THE BLUE SALON DURING THE FAILURE OF THE LIGHTS.**

Jacques saw it.

Pierre saw it.

Armand closed the book before anyone else could read further.

“You said midnight,” Jacques observed.

“I wished to improve the appetite.”

“Whose testimony is that?”

“Mine.”

For the first time that evening, Jacques’s composure shifted. It was a minute change: the stillness of an animal that has heard a twig break.

“You testified at the trial,” Pierre said to Armand. “You said you remained in the servants’ hall throughout the darkness.”

“I lied.”

“You let me convict an innocent man.”

Armand looked at him almost kindly. “No, Inspector. You did that yourself.”

Amélie laughed once, without amusement.

“Shall we count the honest people at the table?” she asked. “François may become lonely.”

“I am not honest,” François said.

The admission silenced them more effectively than a protest would have done.

Armand put the notebook on the sideboard again.

“Sixteen years ago,” he said, “the Marquis held a masked ball in this house. At eleven-twenty, the lights failed for one minute. When they returned, the cabinet in the blue salon was open and the Saint-Auban sapphire was gone. Luc Bellande was found in the corridor with the cabinet key in his pocket.”

François’s fingers tightened around the prison letter.

“The key was planted,” he said.

“Yes.”

Pierre stood so suddenly that his chair scraped the tiles.

“If you possess evidence of that, Valère, you will hand it to me now.”

“You no longer possess a badge.”

“I possess enough authority to prevent the destruction of evidence.”

“You had evidence once. Miss Renaud gave you a statement contradicting Vallon’s alibi. It disappeared.”

Every face turned toward Amélie.

She was looking at Pierre.

“So you kept that too,” she said.

“I kept everything.”

Hélène rose. “And the payments?”

“Your husband’s first payment is recorded on page forty-eight.”

“For what?” François demanded.

Hélène answered before Armand could.

“For silence.”

She sat down again.

Armand lifted the wine bottle but found Pierre’s glass covered by his hand.

“No more theatre,” Pierre said. “Read it now.”

“Theatre is precisely what is required. Memory is vain. It will swear to anything until made to stand again in the room where it lied.”

He went to the terrace doors and laid his hand upon the bolt.

“We shall reconstruct that minute.”

Hélène stared at him. “Have you gone mad?”

“No. Merely old. There is a difference. Amélie has brought the record that was playing. Pierre has his watch. You have brought the paper knife that lay beside the Marquis’s correspondence. Jacques has opened the same cigar box. François has brought the dead man’s letter.”

“And what did you bring?” Jacques asked.

Armand touched the notebook.

“The reason none of you can leave.”

There was a low sound from the hall clock. Half past ten.

Armand looked at Pierre.

“Let us give the past the same doors it had before.”

Pierre hesitated, then crossed the room. He opened both terrace doors.

Cold air entered at once, carrying rain and the smell of rosemary from the garden. Beyond the terrace a covered passage ran along the wall of the house to the study. Pierre stepped outside, examined it, and returned with wet shoulders.

“Clear,” he said. “The study doors are shut.”

“As they were that night.”

“And the road?” Hélène asked.

“Impassable,” said Pierre. “No one has come or gone.”

Armand moved the notebook to the study. Through the open dining-room door they saw him place it in the centre of the desk.

When he returned, Amélie was standing beside the gramophone.

“The last waltz,” she said. “You always did choose sentimental methods for cruel things.”

She set the needle at a chalk mark near the record’s final groove.

“When I raise my handkerchief?”

“The lights will go out for sixty seconds,” said Armand. “Pierre will time them. No one will leave the room.”

Jacques smiled again.

“And after sixty seconds?”

“We shall discover,” said Armand, “whether the past has learned anything.”

### Chapter IV — The Lost Minute

The gramophone began with a whisper of surface noise. Then the strings emerged, thin and distant, followed by a woman’s voice singing of a last dance beside the sea.

The five guests stood around the dining table.

Pierre held his watch.

François remained beside the empty chair, his father’s letter beneath one hand.

Hélène’s face had the stillness of carved ivory. Her eyes moved once toward the hall leading to the study.

Amélie listened to the record. When the singer reached the final refrain, she untied the blue handkerchief from her wrist and lifted it.

Armand extinguished the lights.

Darkness filled the room.

It was not complete. Lightning showed the terrace in sudden white rectangles; the gramophone’s needle caught a glint from somewhere. But between the flashes there was only the music and the cold current of air from the garden.

Pierre’s voice came out of the dark.

“Ten seconds.”

A chair moved.

“Who is that?” François asked.

No one answered.

At twenty seconds Amélie heard footsteps on wet stone. They were careful but not quiet enough to escape a singer’s ear.

At thirty seconds she heard a door open in the study.

Then Jacques Vallon’s voice, stripped of its dinner-table charm:

“Give me page forty-seven.”

Armand answered. He sounded almost pleased.

“A page can burn; my confession cannot.”

There was a sharp cry.

Something struck wood.

Hélène breathed in beside the table. A moment later Amélie heard silk pass close to her and knew that Hélène too had left the room.

Pierre said, “Forty-five seconds.”

François swore under his breath.

From the terrace came the scrape of a shoe.

Lightning broke over the garden. For one instant Pierre saw a man step inside: black evening clothes, a silver flower, pale gloves. Then the room was dark again.

“Sixty.”

The lights returned.

Jacques stood near the terrace doors, one hand on the back of his chair.

Amélie was beside the gramophone.

François stood at the empty place.

Pierre was already moving.

Hélène was gone.

He crossed the hall and entered the study.

Armand lay beside the desk. The silver paper knife rested near his hand, its engraved handle turned upward. Le Carnet Bleu lay open on the blotter.

Hélène stood over the body holding a packet of letters tied with black ribbon. The west drawer of the desk was open. Violet perfume hung in the air.

Pierre stopped on the threshold.

His first instinct was the old one: preserve the room, name the visible facts, trust nothing else.

“Armand is dead,” he called. “Madame de Veyrac is beside him. The west drawer is open. She is holding letters.”

Then his eyes fell on the notebook.

The page on the left was numbered forty-six.

The page on the right was numbered forty-eight.

“And,” Pierre said, more quietly, “page forty-seven is gone.”

### Chapter V — The First Accusation

Hélène did not drop the letters.

She looked down at Armand with an expression too exhausted for innocence and too private for guilt.

François came into the study behind Pierre. When he saw the silver paper knife, his face changed.

“Yours,” he said to Hélène.

“Yes.”

“You threatened him this afternoon.”

“Yes.”

“You left the dining room in the dark.”

“Yes.”

Pierre turned. “Do you wish to add anything that makes your position worse?”

“Not at present.”

Jacques remained at the door. Amélie watched him remove his pale gloves finger by finger.

Pierre knelt beside Armand. The wound had been made by a single thrust. There was no sign of a struggle beyond one overturned chair and a violet-black streak where the victim’s fountain pen had rolled across the blotter.

“No one touches anything,” Pierre said.

“You are retired,” François reminded him.

“Death has not altered the usefulness of fingerprints.”

“Nor the unreliability of your reports,” said Amélie.

He accepted the blow without looking at her.

The telephone in the hall was dead. The storm had taken the line. The lower road was blocked and the upper track, Pierre reported after a brief inspection from the porch, had become a stream of mud. The nearest gendarmerie was eight kilometres away.

They carried Armand to the sofa in the library and returned to the drawing room. No one suggested resuming dinner.

Pierre placed Le Carnet Bleu on a table beneath the chandelier. Beside it he set the empty blue sheath and Hélène’s paper knife, wrapped separately in handkerchiefs.

“The obvious account,” he said, “is that Madame de Veyrac entered the study, killed Valère, opened the drawer, and removed both page forty-seven and these letters.”

“Then why leave the notebook?” Jacques asked.

“She may have been interrupted.”

“By you.”

“Precisely.”

Hélène sat very straight. The packet of letters lay before her.

“I did not kill him.”

“Why did you enter the study?”

“For these.”

“Love letters?”

“Yes.”

“From your husband?”

For the first time, her composure broke. “No.”

François stared at her.

Hélène untied the black ribbon. The uppermost letter was addressed in a firm, uneducated hand.

**Hélène, ma bien-aimée—**

At the bottom was the signature **Luc**.

“My father?” François said.

“Yes.”

“You knew him.”

“I loved him.”

The rain moved across the windows like applause heard from a distant room.

Hélène went on because there was no dignified way to stop.

“My daughter Claire was Luc’s child. My husband discovered it during the investigation. If Luc defended himself fully, the affair would have become public. Claire would have lost her name, her home, everything. The Marquis offered Armand money. He offered Pierre advancement. He offered Luc nothing except the promise that Claire would never know.”

François’s face was white.

“And my father accepted?”

“He withdrew his accusation against Jacques. He would not name me. He believed the truth about the sapphire would still acquit him.”

“But the remaining evidence disappeared.”

Pierre looked at the floor.

François turned on Hélène. “You let him die.”

“Yes.”

It was the simplicity of the answer that stopped him.

Pierre picked up Hélène’s note from the mantelpiece, where he had placed it after the murder.

“She told me before the blackout to open the west drawer if she was delayed. That supports her reason for entering the study.”

“Or proves preparation,” Jacques said.

Amélie leaned toward the table.

“The paper knife,” she said. “When did you last hold it, Hélène?”

“When I passed it to Jacques.”

All eyes turned toward him.

Jacques gave a faint smile.

“And I returned it.”

François shook his head.

“You returned the sheath.”

### Chapter VI — Five Lies

Jacques looked from François to the empty velvet sheath.

“An observant pastry cook.”

“I watched your hand. The handle did not go back.”

“You were across the table.”

“So was I,” said Amélie. “I saw the silver in your left hand.”

“You also once swore I was beside you during the theft.”

“Yes.”

She met his gaze.

“That was the first of my two statements.”

Pierre closed his eyes briefly.

Amélie told them.

Sixteen years earlier she had been twenty-two, celebrated, foolish, and in love with Jacques. When the lights failed at the masked ball, she heard the silver head of his cane strike the blue salon tiles. After the theft, Jacques begged her to say he had never left her side.

She did.

Two days later, when Luc Bellande was arrested, she went to Pierre and retracted the alibi. She described the cane, the footsteps, and Jacques’s absence. Pierre wrote it down.

Then the statement vanished.

“I marked you as confused,” Pierre said. “I wrote that grief and champagne had made your recollection unreliable.”

“The Marquis promised you Marseille,” said Armand’s notebook from the table, though no one had opened it.

Pierre seemed to hear it.

“He promised me promotion. I had a wife ill with tuberculosis and two children. I told myself Luc would be acquitted on the physical evidence. When the cabinet key was found in his pocket, I told myself the court would see it had been planted. When he was convicted, I told myself the appeal would succeed.”

“And when he died?” François asked.

“I stopped telling myself anything.”

“You destroyed the statement.”

“Yes.”

François stood with both hands on the back of a chair.

“That makes four people who killed him.”

“Five,” Hélène said. “Armand knew.”

Jacques remained beside the window.

“And perhaps six. You brought a forged telegram tonight, François.”

The younger man looked at him sharply.

Pierre unfolded the telegram. “Is it forged?”

François said nothing.

“Delorme has not signed his telegrams ‘Alphonse Delorme’ in twenty years,” Pierre said. “He signs only ‘D’. And no examining magistrate announces the collection of evidence in this fashion.”

“Yes,” François said. “I wrote it.”

“Why?”

“Because Armand ignored every letter I sent. I thought if he believed an official was coming, he would open the book before he could sell it or destroy it.”

“And when he called your bluff?” Jacques asked.

“I was angry.”

“Angry enough to kill?”

“Not before I had read page forty-seven.”

“Convenient.”

“No more convenient than pale gloves.”

Pierre looked toward Jacques’s hands. They were bare now.

“During the final lightning flash,” he said, “I saw a man enter from the terrace. He wore pale gloves.”

“Half the servants in France wear pale gloves.”

“There are no servants here.”

“And did you see my face?”

“No.”

“Then you saw what you expected to see. We have already established the quality of your eyesight when a Vallon is concerned.”

Pierre flushed.

Amélie moved to the gramophone. She turned the record by hand until the needle reached the place where the cry had sounded.

“The murder occurred before the final refrain,” she said. “Between thirty and forty-five seconds. Hélène left after the cry.”

“You cannot know that,” said Jacques.

“I know voices. I heard yours in the study.”

“In thunder, through two rooms, with a gramophone playing?”

“You said, ‘Give me page forty-seven.’ Armand answered, ‘A page can burn; my confession cannot.’”

Jacques’s expression did not move.

“A remarkable performance.”

“It is why people once paid to hear me.”

Pierre opened Le Carnet Bleu. The torn edge after page forty-six was fresh and ragged. Page forty-eight began with three entries:

**Payment received from H. de V. — for silence concerning L.B. and the child.**

**Statement of A. Renaud delivered to Inspector Mercier. Later withdrawn from file.**

**J.V. paid first instalment. Violet-black correspondence commenced.**

Below them, Armand had drawn a small blue cross.

“Page forty-seven named the thief,” François said. “Whoever tore it out believed the rest of the book was survivable.”

Hélène looked at Jacques.

“Only one of us had that reason.”

“Did he?” Jacques asked. “Your husband’s payments are on page forty-eight. Pierre’s crime is there. Amélie’s false alibi is there. François’s forgery is on the table. We are all ruined equally.”

“No,” said Amélie. “We are guilty differently.”

She looked toward the empty chair.

“That is the whole point.”

### Chapter VII — The Account in Violet Ink

At midnight, the hall clock struck twelve times.

They listened in silence. It was the hour Armand had chosen for his revelation, and the fact that he was dead did not prevent the house from keeping his appointment.

François opened his father’s letter.

The paper had been folded so often that the creases were nearly transparent. He had known the words since childhood, but he read them aloud now.

> My dear son,
>
> A man can bear being punished for what he has done. What is difficult is to be made into another man’s sin. Do not let them teach you that our name is the worst thing about us. I know who left the salon. I shall not write it here, because they open my letters. Ask the singer why she changed her song. Ask the policeman what he lost. Ask Madame what my silence purchased. And ask Armand Valère why a servant who saw nothing became a rich man.

There was no farewell. The prison authorities had removed the second page.

François put the letter down.

“He knew all of you,” he said. “And still he protected her.”

Hélène looked toward the dark window. “He protected Claire.”

“Did she ever know?”

“No.”

“Then he died for a daughter who does not know his name.”

“Yes.”

“Will you tell her?”

Hélène’s hand closed around the black ribbon.

“Yes,” she said at last.

It was the first promise made that night which anyone believed.

Pierre continued through the notebook. Armand’s entries were not a confession in the ordinary sense. They were an inventory of cowardice. Dates, sums, meetings, the exact phrases people had used when asking him to forget. Hélène’s husband had paid to conceal the affair. Pierre had accepted the promised promotion. Amélie had visited Armand twice, first begging for her false alibi to be supported, then begging for the truth to be produced. Jacques had made regular payments for fifteen years.

The amounts increased whenever his fortunes improved.

“He did not merely witness the theft,” Pierre said. “He made a profession of it.”

“And tonight?” Amélie asked. “Why stop?”

“Perhaps he was not stopping,” said Jacques. “Perhaps this dinner was another demand.”

Pierre examined the final written page. The date was that afternoon.

**Registered packet dispatched to Monsieur Delorme, examining magistrate, Aix. Complete account enclosed. Collection 17:10. Receipt in west pigeonhole.**

François stared at it. “He truly sent a confession.”

“If the entry is true,” Jacques said.

Pierre went to the study. The others followed, leaving Hélène with the body in the library.

The west pigeonhole of Armand’s desk contained a postal receipt. It bore the village stamp, the address of the magistrate’s office, and the time: seventeen minutes past five.

“The post boy came before the storm,” Amélie said. “I passed his bicycle on the lower road.”

“Then page forty-seven was no longer the only proof,” François said.

“The murderer did not know that until Armand told him,” Pierre replied.

They looked at the desk.

The room was almost painfully orderly. A chair lay on its side beside the place where Armand had fallen. His fountain pen rested under the edge of the blotter. The silver paper knife had been removed to the drawing room, but its position remained marked by a chalk line Pierre had drawn around it.

Amélie bent over the blotter.

“What is that?”

Near the ragged notebook lay three tiny flakes of blue paper.

Pierre touched one with the point of a pencil. “From the missing leaf.”

“A page torn out hurriedly,” François said.

“By someone wearing gloves,” said Amélie.

Jacques leaned against the door frame. “Why gloves?”

“Because there is violet ink along the torn edge. Armand’s pen rolled across the page when he fell. Bare fingers would carry the stain.”

They all looked at Jacques’s hands again. Clean.

He spread them.

“As you see.”

Pierre left the study and returned with the pale gloves from the drawing-room chair where Jacques had placed them.

The right glove was spotless.

Across the left thumb ran a faint violet-black line.

No one spoke.

Jacques regarded it with mild interest.

“I opened the cigar box with Armand’s ribbon. Perhaps his ink was on it.”

“The ink on the box is dry,” Pierre said. “This is fresh.”

“Then I touched the notebook at dinner.”

“You did not,” said François.

“You are very certain of everyone’s hands.”

“A baker notices hands.”

Amélie picked up the left glove. Something crackled inside its cuff.

Jacques moved for the first time too quickly.

Pierre caught his wrist.

Amélie turned the glove upside down.

A narrow triangle of blue paper fell onto the desk.

On it, in violet-black ink, were the lower strokes of a capital V and the words:

**—the sapphire in his possession—**

The fragment fitted the torn edge after page forty-six.

“That,” Pierre said, “will be difficult to attribute to the cigar box.”

### Chapter VIII — The Second Murder

Jacques drew his hand away.

For several seconds he appeared less frightened than offended. The room had violated the rules by which he had lived: the rules that money outlasted memory, that elegance could make brutality seem improbable, that a dead servant’s son would remain easier to accuse than a living financier.

“You have a scrap of paper,” he said. “It proves I handled the notebook. It does not prove murder.”

“The weapon was in your possession,” François said.

“According to two confessed liars.”

“Pierre saw your gloves on the terrace,” said Hélène from the doorway.

She had entered without their noticing. She no longer held Luc’s letters. Her face was bare of every social expression.

“According to a corrupt policeman,” Jacques replied.

“Amélie heard your voice.”

“According to a woman who changed her evidence once already.”

“And page forty-seven named you.”

“According to the blackmailer I am supposed to have killed.”

He smiled faintly.

“Do you not see? You have built your case out of people no court would trust.”

“That was your mistake,” said François. “You thought untrustworthy people could not tell the truth.”

Pierre set the paper fragment beside the postal receipt.

“Courts do not require saints. They require corroboration.”

“And what exactly is your sequence, Inspector?”

Pierre looked at the others. He had once assembled a false case against Luc Bellande by excluding everything inconvenient. Now he proceeded more slowly.

“At dinner, Monsieur Vallon learned that page forty-seven contained Armand’s testimony naming him. He had come expecting some form of blackmail, perhaps intending theft, perhaps already prepared for murder. He borrowed Madame de Veyrac’s paper knife and retained it while placing the sheath beside her glass.

“Armand then announced the reconstruction. The open terrace provided an unseen route to the study. During the darkness, Vallon went there. Miss Renaud heard him demand the page. Armand told him the confession had already been sent. Vallon killed him anyway—partly to silence him, partly because fifteen years of fear had become hatred.

“He tore out page forty-seven. Fresh ink from the fallen pen marked both the page and his glove. A fragment lodged in the cuff. He left Madame de Veyrac’s weapon to accuse her and returned by the terrace. I saw the gloves and silver carnation when lightning illuminated him.

“Madame entered afterward for the letters. The cry had already occurred. Miss Renaud heard her leave the dining room only after it. I found her at the desk because she had no reason to flee with what she had come to recover.”

“One flaw,” Jacques said. “If I planned to frame Hélène, why allow her to be found with letters that explain her presence?”

“You did not know the letters were there,” Hélène answered. “You believed I would be found with the knife and the body. Armand’s other secrets complicated yours.”

“A second flaw. Why take one page and leave an entire notebook of payments linking me to Armand?”

“Because payment is not theft,” François said. “Page forty-seven was the witness statement. It was the only entry that placed the sapphire in your hands.”

“Was it?” Jacques glanced at the scrap. “You have not read the sentence.”

Amélie picked it up.

“No,” she said. “But I heard the cane.”

She moved closer to him.

“Do you remember what you told me after the ball? You said you had gone to the terrace because you felt ill. You said the sound I heard was your cane falling beside the door. I believed you because I wanted to. Luc Bellande did not have a cane. He did not know the cabinet key had been planted in his pocket. And he did not have the sapphire.”

Jacques’s smile vanished.

“Armand did not see you leave the salon,” she continued. “He saw you return. That is what the missing sentence says, is it not? He saw the sapphire in your possession.”

The storm was moving east. Thunder sounded farther out to sea.

Jacques looked at François.

“Your father was a fool.”

François went very still.

“He could have saved himself. He had only to name Hélène and the Marquis. He chose a child who was not his legitimate concern.”

“He chose someone he loved.”

“And died.”

“Yes.”

François’s voice remained calm.

“You chose only yourself. We shall see which choice survives the morning.”

Something in Jacques yielded—not conscience, perhaps, but the need to continue pretending that conscience mattered.

He went to the window and looked at the garden passage.

“Armand laughed at me,” he said. “For fifteen years I paid him. Every success increased his price. Every envelope reminded me that my life belonged to a servant with a blue notebook. Tonight he told me he had sent the confession. He had taken the money and ruined me anyway.”

“So you killed him,” Pierre said.

“I had decided before dinner that if the book contained what I feared, I would remove it. When he arranged the darkness, it was as though he had prepared the room for me.”

“And the knife?” Hélène asked.

“You brought it. Armand made certain of that.”

“He knew what you might do.”

“He wanted to see whether I would.”

Amélie looked toward the library where Armand lay.

“Then he staged a second murder,” she said. “His own.”

“No,” said Pierre. “He staged a temptation. Vallon supplied the murder.”

Jacques turned from the window.

“Do not make Armand noble. He could have saved Luc sixteen years ago. Instead he discovered that justice paid badly and secrecy paid every month.”

“That is true,” François said.

Jacques seemed surprised.

“But it does not make you innocent,” François continued. “That is another truth.”

### Chapter IX — The Empty Chair

They locked Jacques in the blue salon.

Pierre kept the key. There were other exits, but none that did not open onto the storm, the cliff, or the mud-blocked road. Jacques made no attempt to escape.

In the dining room, the candles had burned low. The soup remained in its bowls beneath a skin of cold fat. Luc Bellande’s empty chair stood between the living as it had all evening.

François sat in it.

No one objected.

Hélène placed the packet of letters before him.

“They belong to you,” she said.

“No. They belong to Claire.”

“She may not want them.”

“Want has very little to do with inheritance.”

Hélène accepted this. “I shall tell her.”

“All of it?”

“All I can bear.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“No.” She drew a breath. “All of it.”

Pierre had returned to the notebook. “My statement must be written before the police arrive.”

Amélie sat opposite him. “Which statement?”

He looked at her.

“The one I should have written sixteen years ago.”

“You did write it. You destroyed it.”

“Then I shall write that too.”

He took Armand’s violet-black pen. The nib scratched on white paper.

François watched him for a moment, then opened the prison letter again.

“There is something I do not understand,” he said. “Why summon us now? Armand had lived from this account for years.”

Amélie looked toward the sideboard.

There, behind the wine bottles, stood six plates. On the sixth was a small square of almond cake.

“He was ill,” she said.

Hélène nodded. “He told me this afternoon. The doctor had given him three months.”

“So he wished to clear his conscience?” Pierre asked.

“Armand did not possess that kind of conscience.”

François considered. “He wished to choose the ending.”

“Yes,” Hélène said. “He could not bear death making the final entry without him.”

They found his will in the west pigeonhole beneath the postal receipt. Maison Bleue and the money in his accounts were left to François Bellande, “in partial and therefore inadequate restitution.” The notebook was left to the examining magistrate. A separate envelope contained a letter for Claire.

There was nothing for Jacques.

Nothing for Hélène, Pierre, or Amélie.

At the bottom, Armand had written:

**An account is not forgiveness. It is only the refusal to lose the figures.**

François read the line twice.

“He believed this was restitution?”

“He believed money was the only language he had spoken clearly,” Hélène said.

“Do you want the house?” Amélie asked.

François looked around the dining room: the blue shutters, the empty glasses, the chair in which his father had never sat.

“No.”

“What will you do with it?”

“Sell it.”

Hélène’s face tightened, but she said nothing.

“The money will establish a fund for prisoners’ families,” he continued. “And the blue salon will be taken apart. Stone by stone if necessary.”

“Good,” said Amélie.

Pierre kept writing.

At two in the morning the rain stopped.

At three, Amélie opened the terrace doors. The air had changed. The sea was still rough, but the wind carried the clean scent of wet earth rather than the closed breath of the house.

Hélène came to stand beside her.

“Did you love Jacques?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Even tonight?”

Amélie considered the dark garden.

“I loved the man for whom I lied. He may never have existed.”

“That does not make the love unreal.”

“No. Only badly addressed.”

Behind them, François placed his father’s prison letter inside Le Carnet Bleu at the place where page forty-seven had been.

The missing leaf left a narrow blue fringe along the binding.

It no longer looked like an absence.

It looked like a wound.

### Chapter X — Dawn at Maison Bleue

The gendarmes arrived shortly after seven, walking the final kilometre where the road had fallen away.

With them came Monsieur Delorme, the examining magistrate from Aix.

He was a small, impatient man in a mud-splashed coat. François stared at him as though his own forged telegram had summoned its recipient by magic.

“Valère’s registered packet reached my office on the evening post,” Delorme said. “I left before dawn. Which of you is Bellande?”

François raised his hand.

Delorme studied him. “Your father’s conviction will be referred for formal review. I cannot promise speed.”

“He has been dead fifteen years.”

“Sixteen,” Pierre corrected automatically.

Everyone looked at him.

Pierre removed his old police watch and set it on the table.

“Sixteen,” François said. “You see the difficulty with speed.”

Delorme inclined his head.

Jacques came out of the blue salon between two gendarmes. He had recovered his jacket and silver carnation, but not his gloves. Without them, his hands looked older than the rest of him.

He paused beside the empty chair.

“You will all be destroyed by this,” he said. “Do you understand? The affair, the false statements, the payments. None of you leaves with a name worth having.”

Hélène answered him.

“We have tried living with names we did not deserve.”

Jacques looked at Amélie. Whatever he hoped to find in her face was absent.

Then he was taken away.

Delorme collected the notebook, Pierre’s confession, the paper fragment, the gloves, the weapon, the postal receipt, and François’s forged telegram.

“You understand,” he told François, “that forging an official communication is an offence.”

“Yes.”

“Why did you do it?”

“Because the truth had ignored ordinary invitations.”

Delorme’s mouth moved slightly. “We shall discuss it.”

The sun rose behind the house. Light entered the dining room and touched every place at the table, including Luc Bellande’s.

Hélène left first. She carried the letters she would give her daughter.

Pierre walked down to the village beside the gendarmes, his unsigned retirement honours still in his pocket and his confession in Delorme’s case.

Amélie remained at the terrace until François joined her.

“What will you call the fund?” she asked.

“I had not thought.”

“Do not call it Saint-Auban.”

“No.”

“Nor Maison Bleue.”

“No.”

They looked through the open doors at the table.

“The Luc Bellande Fund?” she suggested.

François shook his head.

“He wrote that our name was not the worst thing about us. I do not think he would want it turned into the best.”

Amélie smiled.

“Then what?”

“The Empty Chair.”

Below the headland, the sea had become blue again.

François took his father’s place card from the table and slipped it into his pocket. Then he closed the door of Maison Bleue behind them.

For the first time in sixteen years, nothing remained inside but the truth.
