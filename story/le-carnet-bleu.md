# The Last Recording

> Exploratory prose reference for the validated Maison Bleue demo setting. The structured runtime remains canonical.

## Premise

Fifteen years ago, an unknown pianist named Anaïs Loret sold a working score to the celebrated composer Henri Valois. She believed she was selling an arrangement. Henri registered the entire waltz, **La Dernière Marée**, in his own name.

The song made careers. It also made a private fortune.

Armand Delatour arranged the false registration. He kept a blue notebook recording every royalty payment, private settlement, and act of silence that followed. Now old, compromised, and still incapable of a clean apology, he invites five people to Maison Bleue under five different promises of restitution.

Before midnight, he says, Anaïs will have her name back.

On the dining-room table are five sealed packets—one for each guest—and the blue account of everyone who profited from the stolen song. Colette Dumas places Anaïs’s surviving test recording on the gramophone.

During the second verse, a sixth envelope addressed privately to Armand appears on top of his notebook.

He opens it and pricks his thumb on the fictional poisoned sliver hidden beneath the inner fold.

He collapses before the final refrain.

## The five claimants

### Mathilde Loret — the heir

Mathilde is Anaïs’s granddaughter and a forensic music archivist. She has spent years proving that the famous waltz was written before Henri Valois ever saw it.

She comes carrying a ribbon-bound working score. Its paper, ink, and corrections predate Henri’s registration by four months.

Mathilde also comes carrying rage. Earlier that day she sent Armand a note:

> Return her name tonight, or I will make certain yours survives only in disgrace.

Armand’s invitation told Mathilde that Anaïs’s original score would be waiting in the study’s west drawer. After he collapses, she immediately takes it—or openly directs Rémy to retrieve it for seated play—because she does not trust another promise of safekeeping. Her threat, her handwriting, and that retrieval make her the obvious first suspect.

### Gabriel Valois — the inheritor

Gabriel publishes the catalogue that bears his father’s name. He inherited prestige, employees, contracts, and a lie.

Two years ago, Gabriel found Henri’s private purchase agreement. It describes Anaïs’s payment as a purchase of “manuscript and silence.” Since then, Gabriel has anonymously sent Mathilde half of his personal royalties. He has never admitted why.

His packet contains a current dividend statement. It reveals that the Valois family no longer receives most of the song’s income. Sixty percent flows to a quiet rights company called Éditions du Méridien.

### Colette Dumas — the voice

Colette sang the first test recording and the public premiere. On the surviving test pressing, before the waltz begins, her younger voice says:

> Anaïs Loret at the piano. One more time from the bridge.

Henri Valois was not in the room.

Colette knew the melody was Anaïs’s. She performed anyway because the premiere was the first role that could make her famous. She later told Mathilde that the test pressing had been destroyed.

Tonight she brings it back.

### Rémy Fauré — the critic

Rémy wrote the only contemporary review that questioned the song’s authorship. Before publication, Armand offered him a prestigious editorship. Rémy withdrew the review and built a career on the bargain.

Armand names him neutral executor of the restitution packets. Rémy counts exactly five, shows each name to the table, and places them in a row away from the blue notebook.

He also carries a registry extract linking Éditions du Méridien to its true owner.

### Solange Béraud — the solicitor

Solange administers the rights with immaculate calm. She presents herself as the only person in the room capable of separating justice from sentiment.

In truth, Solange secretly owns Éditions du Méridien through two nominee companies. For fifteen years, the shell publisher has taken most of the royalties from the stolen waltz.

Armand’s packet for Solange contains a transfer of the publisher and its catalogue income to Anaïs Loret’s estate.

If the transfer is executed, Solange loses the company. If the blue notebook is read, she loses much more.

## The opening

Armand waits until every guest is seated before placing the five named packets on the table.

“Each of you was promised something different,” he says. “These are not bargains. They are restitution.”

Rémy counts the sealed packets.

“Five,” he says. “One for each guest. No substitutions.”

He places them in a straight row, well clear of Armand’s notebook.

Mathilde places Anaïs’s working score beside the notebook and reads the date aloud.

Gabriel opens his packet. He reads the dividend split, first with disbelief and then again without it.

“Éditions du Méridien,” he says. “Sixty percent.”

Solange folds her hands. “A standard catalogue vehicle.”

“Whose?” Mathilde asks.

Solange does not answer. Four packets remain sealed beside Gabriel’s open one.

Colette moves to the gramophone.

“You have all heard Henri’s waltz,” she says. “Now hear Anaïs’s.”

The test recording begins with surface noise. Then comes Colette’s young voice naming Anaïs. A piano sounds from fifteen years away—unpolished, intimate, and unmistakably the same melody.

Everyone turns toward the gramophone.

Solange places a sixth envelope on top of Armand’s notebook.

It is a safe, empty prop in play. In the fiction, a poisoned glass sliver is hidden beneath its inner fold. A blue carbon-copy label in Mathilde’s actual hand reads “Armand Delatour — private”. The prop contains no glass, point, or substance.

Armand notices the envelope during the second verse. He recognises Mathilde’s hand, assumes it answers his restitution, and opens it. He mimes the hidden sliver pricking his thumb.

Before the final refrain, he performs the rehearsed seated collapse.

Colette stops the recording and lays her silver-blue handkerchief beside the gramophone.

On that prearranged cue, while Armand remains still, Mathilde goes to the staged study and retrieves the separate replica of Anaïs’s original score from the west drawer. For seated play, she openly directs Rémy to retrieve it as her named proxy. Either way, the score returns in full view rather than being hidden.

Rémy accounts for the visible facts:

- all five named packets are accounted for—four sealed and Gabriel’s open;
- a sixth envelope addressed privately to Armand lies open on the blue notebook;
- the blue address label is genuinely in Mathilde’s hand;
- Mathilde has returned from the study carrying the disputed score.

Rémy then reads the evidence card from the already-open safe envelope. Only after he finishes does Armand sit up and become Game Master.

The four still-sealed packets may now be opened:

- Mathilde receives Armand’s signed correction naming Anaïs as sole composer and assigning her estate the authorship share, subject to the current publisher’s countersignature;
- Colette receives the original studio log naming Anaïs at the piano and a release from Armand’s confidentiality promise;
- Rémy receives Armand’s signed admission that the editorship was payment for withdrawing his review;
- Solange receives the transfer that would surrender Éditions du Méridien and its catalogue income to Anaïs’s estate.

Gabriel’s already-open packet contains Henri’s “manuscript and silence” contract and the current dividend statement.

The investigation begins.

## What actually happened

Anaïs Loret composed **La Dernière Marée**. Henri Valois purchased her working papers, altered the title page, and registered the waltz as his own. Armand arranged the paperwork. Colette accepted fame while knowing the truth. Rémy withdrew the only review that challenged it. Gabriel inherited the result.

Solange turned the old fraud into a current one.

Through Éditions du Méridien, she diverted the majority of the royalties to herself. Armand recorded those payments in the blue notebook. His restitution plan would have transferred the publisher to Anaïs’s estate in front of witnesses.

Solange arrived prepared to kill him and frame Mathilde. The transfer inventory told her that Mathilde expected Anaïs’s original score in the west drawer, giving the frame a predictable final movement.

Before dinner, she asked Mathilde to write “Armand Delatour — private” on an archive label over blue carbon. She returned the top sheet, kept the duplicate, and trimmed it to fit the murder envelope. Colette saw her trim the blue copy but accepted her explanation that it belonged to the seating plan.

Solange fictionally hid a poisoned glass sliver beneath the sixth envelope’s inner fold and attached Mathilde’s carbon-copy label. During the recording, while the room faced the gramophone, she placed the safe prop on Armand’s notebook.

Rémy’s count proves the envelope was not among the original packets.

Gabriel saw Solange lean over the notebook.

Colette saw Solange watching Armand’s hands instead of listening to the recording.

The address label is missing one corner. A torn blue fragment in Solange’s folio fits it exactly.

The company registry supplies the motive. The sixth envelope supplies the act. The carbon fragment supplies the frame.

Mathilde’s dash to the study is a theft, but not a murder. She did exactly what she had threatened to do: she took Anaïs’s name out of Armand’s hands.

## Reveal

Rémy lays the five packets in their original row.

Then he places the sixth envelope beneath them.

“This is not restitution,” he says. “It is an addition.”

Solange looks at Mathilde. “An addition in her hand.”

Rémy points to the torn blue corner already protruding from Solange’s open folio. No one needs to seize or search another player’s prop.

“Almost,” she says.

The broken edge fits the carbon-copy address label. The same missing corner, the same paper fibres, the same impression preserved on the archive label Mathilde wrote before dinner.

Gabriel unfolds the company registry.

“Éditions du Méridien has one beneficial owner.”

Colette restarts the test pressing. Anaïs’s piano fills the room while Solange’s careful corporate structure is read aloud.

For fifteen years, everyone at Maison Bleue had treated the stolen song as a question of memory, shame, or taste.

The sixth envelope makes it a murder.

The blue account makes it arithmetic.

And the recording gives the music back its name.
